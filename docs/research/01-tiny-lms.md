# Survey 1 — The best dense LM under 10M parameters and its cost in bytes

Consolidated from web research on 2026-09-30.

> **Framed for thesis v0.1.** Its "Minimum experiment" section predates [concept v0.2](../concept.md); E0–E4 are defined only by the [roadmap](../roadmap.md).

## Question

What can the best dense LM with ≤10M parameters do today, and how many bytes does it cost, i.e. what is
the dense frontier that a 1 474 560-byte floppy can host in full (weights + tokenizer + runtime)?

## What exists

**TinyStories** (Eldan & Li 2023, [arXiv:2305.07759](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759)) is the mandatory
starting point: a synthetic corpus of stories with the vocabulary of a 3–4-year-old child (about 1500
base words) generated by GPT-3.5/GPT-4, on which GPT-Neo models from ~1M to ~33M parameters produce
multi-paragraph stories that are "fluent, consistent and with almost perfect grammar". The tokenizer is
GPT-Neo's, reduced to the 10K most frequent tokens, context 512. The central claim, verified by reading
the paper, is that grammar emerges early while consistency and creativity need more capacity:
consistency with the beginning of the story appears when the hidden size goes from 64 to 128, depth
matters more for context tracking and width more for facts. On the single "Lucy and the ladder" example
GPT-4 assigns (grammar/creativity/consistency, out of 10): 1M at 8 layers 6/3/2, 2.5M at 8 layers 5/6/3,
8.3M at 8 layers 7/5/5, 28M at 8 layers 9/6/9, 21M at 1 layer 8/–/7. The 1M model answers none of the
factual prompts correctly and often produces ungrammatical sentences; the 28M beats GPT2-XL (1.5B) on
these prompts. These numbers are per-example, not averages: read them as orders of magnitude. One
detail is decisive for us: the "1M/3M/8M" counts of the HF checkpoints are not byte-realistic.
`TinyStories-1M` has hidden 64, 8 layers and `vocab_size` 50257 in its config, so the embedding matrix
alone is ~3.2M parameters; the repository weighs 51.9 MB and `tokenizer.json` alone weighs 2.11 MB, more
than the whole floppy.

**llama2.c** (Karpathy, [github.com/karpathy/llama2.c](https://github.com/karpathy/llama2.c)) is the
reference closest to our artifact because it cleanly separates checkpoint, tokenizer and runtime:
`run.c` is ~700 lines of pure C in float32, `runq.c` adds int8 Q8_0 (4× smaller checkpoints, ~3×
faster). The published TinyStories models are 260K (dim 64, 5 layers, 8 heads, 4 kv, ctx 512,
val loss 1.297), 15M (dim 288, 6 layers, val loss 1.072), 42M (0.847) and 110M (0.760). The 260K
([doc/stories260K.md](https://github.com/karpathy/llama2.c/blob/master/doc/stories260K.md)) uses a
custom 512-token tokenizer (`tok512.bin` 6.23 KB) and weighs 1.06 MB in fp32: in int8 it would be
~260 KB, already inside the floppy with a wide margin; it produces grammatical but repetitive stories at
temperature 0. The 15M weighs ~60 MB fp32, ~15 MB int8, ~7.5 MB at 4 bits: out of budget by a factor of
~5. Karpathy also notes that a 4096-token tokenizer trained on TinyStories gives sequences as long as
those of the 32K Llama 2 tokenizer: at tiny scale a large vocabulary is almost pure cost.

**SimpleStories** (2025, [arXiv:2504.09184](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09184)) is the most relevant
direct successor: 2M parameterized stories in English and Japanese and a suite of models of 1.25M
(4 layers, d 128), 5M (6, 256), 11M (6, 384), 30M and 35M, all with a 4096-token morphological WordPiece
tokenizer. The authors claim to "push the frontier of the model with the fewest parameters that produces
grammatical natural language" and report that their models beat TinyStories-33M on all metrics of a
GPT-4o-mini judge (N=200), while admitting that the true lower bound remains open. Accounting note: with
V=4096 and d=128 the embedding of the 1.25M model is ~0.52M parameters, i.e. almost half the model.

**Super Tiny Language Models** (Hillier, Guertler et al. 2024,
[arXiv:2405.14159](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14159)) is a research program rather than a result:
byte-level tokenization with pooling, weight tying and efficient training to cut parameters by 90–95%,
targeting 10M/50M/100M. It confirms the direction (vocabulary is the first enemy) but provides no
measured frontier point at 10M.

**MobileLLM** (Liu et al., ICML 2024, [arXiv:2402.14905](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14905)) establishes
at sub-billion scale three rules that apply to us a fortiori: deep and thin beats wide and shallow;
share input and output embeddings (in the 125M this saves ~16M parameters, 11.8%, at −0.2 accuracy
points, recoverable by spending the parameters on depth); grouped-query attention. The MobileLLM-LS
variant repeats each block twice (block-wise weight sharing) and gains another 0.7 points at +2.6%
latency: it is already an elementary form of "procedural description expanded at runtime", and must be
treated as such (see Implication).

**SmolLM2-135M** (Allal et al. 2025, [arXiv:2502.02737](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02737)) is the upper
reference: 30 layers, hidden 576, vocabulary 49 152 with tied embeddings (~28M embedding-only
parameters, ~21% of the total), trained on ~2T tokens. In bf16 it weighs ~270 MB, at 4 bits still
~70–100 MB: two orders of magnitude above the floppy. It only serves as an anchor for "what you get when
budget is not the constraint".

**Ternary.** BitNet b1.58 (Ma et al. 2024, [arXiv:2402.17764](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17764))
shows weights in {−1,0,+1} at 1.58 bits matching fp16 LLaMA at equal parameters and tokens, but the
table says parity arrives only at 3B (700M: ppl 12.87 vs 12.33). Spectra/TriLM (Kaushal et al. 2024,
[arXiv:2407.12327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.12327)) confirms that ternary wins at equal bits only
above ~1B, with the suite starting at 99M. Nielsen et al. ([arXiv:2411.05882](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05882))
instead find parity or better already on MLPs, GNNs and small transformers, with a possible
regularizing effect. At microcontroller scale, Atome LM ([github.com/TilelliLab/atome-lm](https://github.com/TilelliLab/atome-lm), 2026) packs a byte-level (V=256) 944K ternary model into a 271 KB blob plus a 2.6 KB C99 engine,
but honestly documents that at 944K the fp32 baseline wins by ~11% and that the model is "sometimes
incoherent". The most recent work, "Baseline Shape Decides the Verdict" (September 2026,
[arXiv:2609.29397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29397)), reanalyzes the alleged 22% advantage of routed
ternary blocks at 60K parameters and shows that, at a fixed byte budget, shape alone (depth/width) of
transformers at equal parameters moves the loss by 22.6%: the advantage vanishes against a well-sized
baseline. On the storage side, BITCOS ([arXiv:2609.16338](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16338)) exploits
zero density (up to 51.5%) to get down to 1.485 bits/weight, below the 1.585 limit of ternary packing.

**Precision and "bigger but smaller".** Kumar et al., "Scaling Laws for Precision"
([arXiv:2411.04330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04330)), model low precision as a reduction of
"effective parameters" and observe that post-training quantization degradation grows with training
data: the dense model must be trained quantization-aware, not quantized afterwards. VBQ
([arXiv:2607.02893](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02893), 2026) learns the precision per group of 64
weights in {1,2,4,8} bits (69% collapse to 1 bit) and on TinyStories a 131M at 1.82 average bits reaches
the perplexity of a 55M fp16 with 3.8× less storage. SeedLM (Apple 2024,
[arXiv:2410.10714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10714)) encodes weight blocks as LFSR seeds plus a few
coefficients, at 3–4 bits, on Llama 3 70B. These are the two works closest to the FloppyLM thesis and
must be cited as prior art for F0.

**BabyLM strict-small** ([findings 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.babylm-main.28/),
[arXiv:2504.08165](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08165)) constrains the _data_ (10M words), not the
parameters: the winners (GPT-BERT) are models with tens of millions of parameters. It is relevant to the
evaluation pipeline (Survey 5), not to the byte frontier. Regional TinyStories
([arXiv:2504.07989](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07989)) confirms at 5–10M that language-specific
tokenizers beat generic ones.

**The tokenizer as a cost.** At this scale the embedding dominates. With d=192 the tied matrix costs
49K parameters at V=256 (byte-level), 98K at V=512, 393K at V=2048, 786K at V=4096, 1.9M at V=10K
(TinyStories) and 9.6M at V=50 257 (GPT-Neo): with a budget of ~2.8M parameters at 4 bits, V=4096
already consumes 28% of the model and V≥10K is simply impossible. The tokenizer file adds ~6 KB at
V=512 and a few tens of KB at V=4096; byte-level costs zero but lengthens sequences (≈1 byte/token vs
≈2–4) and therefore the inference cost and the effective context. The reasonable compromise is a
512–2048-token BPE/unigram trained on the corpus, with tied embeddings.

**The 1.44 MB frontier, in numbers.** A FAT12-formatted floppy has 1 474 560 raw bytes but, after the
boot sector, two 9-sector FATs and a 14-sector root directory, 1 457 664 allocatable bytes remain.
Reserving 10–40 KB for the static C runtime (order of magnitude of `run.c` compiled with `-Os`) and
≤10 KB for the tokenizer leaves ~1.40 MB ≈ 11.2 Mbit for the weights. This gives: ~1.4M parameters in
int8; ~2.5M at 4 bits with fp16 scales per group of 32 (4.5 bits/weight) or ~2.8M with per-row scales;
~7.0M ternary packed at 5 trits/byte (1.6 bits/weight) and ~7.5M with zero-exploiting coding. A
plausible dense configuration at 4 bits is d=192, 5 layers, tied V=2048 (~2.6M parameters); in ternary
d=320, 5 layers (~6.4M, embedding at 4 bits). In quality, 4 bits falls between TinyStories 2.5M and 8.3M
and close to SimpleStories 1.25M–5M: good grammar (≈6–7/10), weak-to-medium consistency (≈3–5/10), no
reliable factual knowledge. In bpb, from the llama2.c val losses (1.072 nat/token for the 15M with
~4 bytes/token estimated ⇒ ~0.38 bpb; 1.297 for the 260K with ~2.2 bytes/token estimated ⇒ ~0.85 bpb)
the ~2.6M frontier should fall around 0.5–0.65 bpb on the TinyStories validation set; the bytes/token
ratio is our own unpublished estimate and must be measured in E0.

## What is missing

The literature has no published dense model with complete "weights + tokenizer + runtime" accounting
under 1.44 MB and TinyStories quality declared and measured in bpb: papers report parameters (often
without embeddings, à la Kaplan) or per-token loss with different tokenizers, never bytes on disk. We
explicitly searched for counter-examples to the thesis: (1) a dense LM ≤1.44 MB that already produces
coherent stories at TinyStories-28M level, and we did not find one (Atome 944K/271 KB is the closest but
describes itself as sometimes incoherent and under-trained, 3000 steps); (2) evidence that a larger
model at fewer bits beats a smaller dense one at equal bytes under 10M, and we found only evidence above
50M (VBQ, Spectra, BitNet) plus contrary evidence at 60K–944K (Baseline Shape, Atome); (3)
procedural/seed-based weight descriptions (SeedLM, MobileLLM-LS weight sharing) applied to sub-10M
models: none. Also missing are public, comparable bpb numbers for the TinyStories/llama2.c checkpoints,
and a clean validation split (Survey 5: ~30% of the TinyStories validation set appears in train).

## Implication for the thesis

F0 (novelty): the thesis is not new in the abstract. VBQ already demonstrates "bigger-but-smaller" on
TinyStories and SeedLM encodes weights as pseudo-random seeds; MobileLLM-LS is expansion by repetition.
The only defensible novelty is the combination "total accounting on a physical medium + sub-10M regime +
procedural expansion at boot", and it must be stated as such. F1 (procedural ≤ dense frontier at equal
bits): the target to beat is not "2.9M at 4 bits" in the abstract but the best among int8 ~1.4M, 4-bit
~2.6M and ternary ~6–7M, each with optimized shape (depth/width/vocabulary): Baseline Shape shows that a
badly chosen shape hands the competitor up to 22% of loss, so a non-optimized baseline invalidates any
win. Block-wise weight sharing belongs to the procedural class but must also be a baseline, because it
is the cheapest possible procedural description. F2 (advantage that vanishes with entropy coding and
pruning on the baseline): this is the most concrete risk, because ternary weights have up to 51% zeros
and BITCOS or arithmetic coding of quantized weights recover 5–15% of bytes for free; every lossless
weight-compression technique applied to the procedural model must be applied identically to the dense
one. F3 (boot cost): the dense frontier has no expansion cost, so every second of boot is a pure cost of
the procedural model; a ~2.6M dense model in C runs at hundreds of tok/s on an i7-1165G7, so the
≥5 tok/s constraint only weighs on the expanded model, not on the baseline. F4 (coherence): at 2.6M the
dense model is at the lower limit of TinyStories coherence (coherence emerges from hidden ≥128 and at
~8M reaches ~5/10); the procedural model must therefore expand to an effective model ≥10–30M to have a
visible advantage, which makes F3 binding.

## Minimum experiment

E0 builds the dense frontier with real byte accounting: three families (int8, 4 bits with groups,
ternary QAT) on deduplicated and re-split TinyStories, BPE tokenizers of 256/512/1024/2048, shape sweep
at a fixed budget of 11.2 Mbit, same number of training tokens for all; it measures held-out bpb, bytes
of the weights file + tokenizer + `run.c`-like runtime, and publishes the bpb-vs-bytes curve, not a
point. E1 trains the procedural model (seed + coefficients, sharing, generator) at the same bit budget
and the same compute, and compares it on the E0 curve. E2 applies the same lossless weight-compression
pipeline to all (pruning, entropy/rANS coding, possibly a rate loss during training to reduce weight
entropy) and recomputes the frontier: if E1's advantage vanishes, F2 is confirmed. E3 scales the
expanded effective parameters (10M, 30M, 100M) measuring expansion time, peak RSS and tok/s on the
i7-1165G7 to trace where F3 cuts. E4 writes the real 1 474 560-byte FAT12 image with static runtime,
tokenizer and weights, boots it from scratch on a clean machine and repeats the E0–E3 measurements on
the image contents alone.
